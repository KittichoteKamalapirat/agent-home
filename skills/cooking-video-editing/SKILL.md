# Cooking video editing

Follow the user's cooking-edit preferences below. Build rhythm around visible actions and preserve recipe coverage. A newer instruction or approved example takes precedence.

## 1. Establish recipe coverage

Read the supplied recipe and separate its components before selecting shots. Keep a coverage ledger beside the editing artifacts with these columns:

| Component | Ingredient or action | Source file and time range | Selected timeline range | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use `included`, `missing footage`, `uncertain identity`, or `user-approved omission` as statuses. Search the footage for unresolved entries. Label an ingredient only when the recipe and footage support its identity.

Treat each ingredient addition in each component as a separate event. Salt in a marinade does not cover salt in a finishing sauce. Record when each component is used: before cooking, during cooking, or after cooking. Use those roles in labels when components look alike, such as "Marinade · before cooking" and "Sauce · after cooking". A step timeline gives the overview; these labels explain the components' different purposes.

Completion criterion: every recipe ingredient and required action has a ledger row, and every unresolved row has an explicit status rather than an assumed inclusion.

## 2. Find the action beats

Scrub candidate footage around the action, then inspect its beginning, contact or release, and result. Use broad thumbnail sampling to find candidates; use adjacent frames to choose edit points.

| Action | Required selection |
| --- | --- |
| Chopping or cutting | Include the first downward knife cut and the last downward knife cut. Jump over repetitive middle cuts. Keep contact and enough of the resulting separation to make both cuts legible. |
| Mixing | Include the ingredient leaving the spoon or fork and entering the container, then a brief mixing action. The transfer is a distinct beat from stirring. |
| Avocado preparation | Default to four ordered beats: split into halves, split into quarters, scoop the flesh, deposit it into the plate or bowl. One complete scoop-and-deposit can convey the last two beats. Follow a newer user-approved cut when it compresses these stages. |
| Plating | Show one complete deposit of each new component, such as rice, meat, tomatoes, and guacamole. Keep the food leaving the utensil and landing. Apply the repetition test below before retaining another spoonful of the same component. |
| Grilling or searing | Begin with the first piece entering the pan or grill. Include its initial contact before moving to turning or browning. |
| Sauce additions | Give every recipe addition a visible pour, sprinkle, squeeze, or drop before blending or mixing. Small additions still count toward coverage. Cut straight to the moment the ingredient leaves the spoon or bottle and lands in the bowl. Skip the reach, the jar opening and the pour into the spoon. |
| Hand mixing or tossing | One continuous shot of about two full mixes, not several jump cuts of the same toss. |
| Pan additions | Each new component going into the pan (marinated meat, vegetables, sauce) gets its moment of landing in the pan. |
| Blending | Start on visible blending and hold long enough to see the contents moving. A shot of an idle blender does not establish this action. Give blending more time when a sub-second flash is hard to read. |
| Filling molds | Choose a clean, complete deposit into a middle cavity when available, with the tray already recognizable. The first cavity is not automatically the strongest shot. |
| Unmolding | Include the cube releasing from the mold and becoming visibly separate. Holding an already released cube does not cover the release. Check each mixture's release separately. |
| Vacuum sealing | When the contraction is the payoff, keep the bag's full-to-fully-vacuumed change. Speed up the continuous action, around 5× when suitable, while keeping both end states readable. |

Completion criterion: each selected range contains its required action and visible result. Every applicable first/last pair and multi-step preparation is represented or marked unavailable in the ledger.

## 3. Assemble around those beats

Set each in-point just early enough to establish the approaching knife, utensil, or ingredient. Set the out-point after contact, release, or separation is visible. Remove waiting and repeated motions between these events. Let the necessary beats determine the total duration unless the user supplies a hard limit.

Use one complete example per action by default. For every repeat, identify the new information it adds: a different ingredient, a distinct preparation stage, a visible texture change, or a necessary final state. Another avocado scoop, sauce spoonful, scrape, or stir usually adds no new information. Keep the strongest complete action and jump to the next stage. Preserve the first/last chopping pair specified above. Ingredient coverage requires each distinct addition, not every repeated quantity of it.

For a recipe short, a simple addition often takes 0.7–0.8 s from just before contact through the landing. A two-mix hand toss runs about 2 s. Blending, cube release, and a complete vacuum contraction can need longer. The opening dish shot can run about 1.2 s under the title, and the final dish holds about 2 s under the ending card. These are starting points; the action and result determine the cut.

Trim waiting and bag handling around the selected actions. Preserve a sealing shot's full contraction when that is the selected payoff. Spend recovered time on readable deposits, pan contact, texture changes, and the finished food. Extend a rushed action through its visible result before cutting another unique step. Judge duration by what the viewer can understand; previous runtime and trim estimates are not targets.

Show waiting times explicitly when they explain the process. Use the recipe's or user's confirmed duration, such as "Freeze for 24 hours", rather than just "Freeze". Keep that duration specific to the recipe.

Maintain the preparation order within each component. Preserve useful knife impacts, drops, and sizzles when natural sound is part of the brief. Check the vertical crop across the whole selected range so the contact point, utensil, food, and receiving container remain readable.

When the user wants editable delivery, use separate clips linked to original sources with available trim handles. When inspecting a user-adjusted project for lessons, leave its cuts intact and distinguish directly observed changes from the user's stated preferences.

Completion criterion: all included ledger entries map to actual timeline clips; every clip has a readable action; any requested editability has been exercised in the target editor and restored afterward.

## 4. Audit before delivery

Watch the assembled sequence at normal speed, then inspect each cut boundary. Check every applicable row in the action table and reconcile every ingredient against the ledger. Repair shots that end before the food lands or the knife completes its cut. Report missing footage and uncertain ingredients explicitly.

Make a pacing pass for shots that are too short to read, linger after their result, or repeat information. For each flagged range, record its timestamp, visible evidence, and a specific keep, shorten, extend, or remove recommendation. Check the share of time spent on preparation versus the main food payoff. If tools permit only sampled frames or scrubbing, state that limit instead of claiming a continuous playback or audio review.

If captions are requested, attach them to the corresponding visible addition and its component. Otherwise leave captions for the user's later pass.

## 5. On-screen ingredient cards

When the user asks for ingredient text, follow these defaults:

- **One card per component**, listing all of that component's ingredients at once: the marinade, the vegetable prep, the sauce, the stir-fry oil and aromatics. Don't show one ingredient per shot. When a component spans several shots, keep its card on screen across them, and highlight the item being added in each shot.
- **Highlights move down the list.** Order the card to match the selected footage, or reorder the footage when the recipe permits. Check the highlight sequence from beginning to end. Keep uncertain identities explicit; use a group label only when the available evidence supports the group.
- **Amount and ingredient only**, such as "1 tbsp soy sauce" or "5g ginger". Leave out preparation words like "grated" or "sliced", because the footage shows them. Name meat generally ("Beef") unless the user wants the cut named.
- **Use the amounts the user actually used.** List only ingredients that went in. When the user changed the source recipe (swapped meat, added chilies, skipped black pepper), the cards and captions follow what the user did, not the original recipe.
- **Pan additions get a short label too** ("Beef mix", "Pepper mix", "Sauce") when each component goes into the pan.
- Use large, centered text with a black outline as the default for cooking labels and ingredient cards. Check the actual phone-sized export over the brightest shot. A gradient or shadow can supplement the outline. Place the card so the action remains visible.

## 6. Footage checks that are easy to miss

- **Check every clip's shape and rotation before cutting.** iPhone clips can be portrait or landscape, with rotation of -90 or -180. Crop a landscape clip to 9:16; never scale it into 9:16, which squashes it. Some ffmpeg 7 builds carry the source rotation onto frames that are already rotated, so reset it after encoding (`ffmpeg -display_rotation 0 -i seg.mp4 -c copy out.mp4`) and check a decoded frame.
- **Colour casts:** a clip lit by a red stove light needs a channel-mix correction to match its neighbours. Compare it side by side with an adjacent clip.
- **Camera choice:** compare overhead and side angles for exposure and action visibility before selecting a shot. Prefer the clearer overhead take when the side angle is blown out. Check the final crop for repeated or doubled food caused by compositing or background fill.
- **Final shot under the app ending card:** the food must sit above the card. Crop and zoom the last shot if needed.

Completion criterion: the action, ingredient, and pacing audits pass; each retained repeat has a stated purpose; remaining omissions are disclosed; and the saved project or export has been reopened and checked. For review-only requests, report findings and preserve the user's edit. Report the checks actually performed.

## 7. Reusable step timeline

For Piiman cooking videos, reuse the approved jumping step timeline unless the user requests another treatment. Choose short labels for the actual recipe stages and align arrivals with their footage. Keep the overview clear beside ingredient cards and below platform controls.

Find the approved mascot assets in the repository before creating new artwork. Search for `piiman-planted-idle` and read its asset README for the editable source, runtime file, preview, and rebuild commands. Keep the asset's detailed settings in that README rather than duplicating them here. If the asset is unavailable in the current checkout, report that gap before substituting a different animation.

Use the approved planted idle while Piiman rests on a step and the existing hops when it changes steps. The idle sways above a fixed bottom pivot and fixed shadow. Preserve the selected pace; horizontal travel belongs to the step transition. When changing speed, preserve the motion range and compare rendered previews before applying it to the video.

Save reusable animation sources, native Rive exports, previews, and rebuild notes in the project repository. Keep large footage and finished video exports with the video's editing artifacts. Treat runtime export restrictions separately from clean native preview captures; inspect what the saved runtime actually includes before recommending it for an app.

Completion criterion: each timeline stage matches the edit; resting and hopping frames are checked at output scale; the idle stays rooted; and the saved source and runtime can be located from the repository documentation.
